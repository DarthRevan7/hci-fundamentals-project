
# Interview 5 - Transcript and Salient Points

### Dialogue Transcript

**Interviewer:** All right, first of all, good afternoon and good evening. Thank you for your availability and for dedicating some of your time to us. As we already mentioned, we are students from Politecnico di Torino conducting a university project to explore how daily life changes during retirement, aiming to better understand habits, daily rhythms, and the moments of a typical day. Naturally, we want to reassure you: this is not an examination and it is not an evaluation; there are no right or wrong answers. We are here solely to listen and learn from your perspective and direct experience. The interview should take about ten minutes; we only ask for your permission to record the audio so that we do not have to take continuous written notes. All collected information will be handled anonymously and used exclusively for academic purposes for this study project. Please feel free to skip any question if you do not feel like answering, or to pause or stop the interview at any time. Do you have any questions or concerns before we begin?

**Retiree:** No, let's go, let's go.

**Interviewer:** All right. So, just to set the context a little: would you like to tell us a bit about yourself and walk us through what a typical day looks like for you, from morning to evening?

**Retiree:** Who knows what a typical day is... I don't know, I'm someone who doesn't do very much. I do very little around the house, just what I need: the housework that I detest, but I have to do it. And then I go out. Non-negotiably, I buy the paper every day, at the very least to read something. And then what do I do on a typical day? I might go for a walk somewhere, I might go to the cinema occasionally. During the winter, I try to take a course designed for retirees, in the sense that it might be held in the afternoon—things I couldn't afford time-wise when I was still working. I go out very little in the evening, practically not at all, because going out at night doesn't interest me anymore. And then a bit of physical exercise, which could be Pilates or even just walking.

**Interviewer:** And is there something you particularly enjoy doing during the week, or nothing in particular?

**Retiree:** There are things I like, but then I might not do them very frequently: I'm also fairly lazy, so I don't dedicate myself much. I also enjoy trying to draw, or watercolor, but I don't learn much because I would need to do it systematically, and I eventually lose consistency. Since retiring, I devote myself much more to doing nothing; I'm not one of those hyper-busy people who constantly have to be doing something.

**Interviewer:** That is an entirely respectable choice.

**Retiree:** Whether it is or not, that's just how it is, and it works for me!

**Interviewer:** Thinking back to earlier stages of your life, what has changed the most since you retired?

**Retiree:** Not having to set an alarm in the morning! That was an exceptional milestone. It might sound like a joke, but it's true: never having to set an alarm clock again was a complete liberation for me.

**Interviewer:** Did you find your past job strenuous or repetitive?

**Retiree:** It was boring for most of the time, except for a brief period when I changed occupations: I enjoyed that one more, but it was also vastly more exhausting. At a certain point I quit because I couldn't handle it anymore, and I thought: "Enough, let's go back to doing a boring job, but one that at least lets you rest on Sundays or do something else instead of being busy all the time."

**Interviewer:** And besides work, what else changed?

**Retiree:** The fact that you choose whether you feel like doing something or you don't. You no longer have things pressuring you; or rather, certain chores still exist, but everything is far more relaxed. At least for me, having no particular family obligations or anything else, at the moment that's how it is: I can choose whether to sit on the couch looking outside or do something.

**Interviewer:** Turning to social relationships: how do your connections usually take shape throughout the week, between phone calls, in-person meetups, or time spent on your own?

**Retiree:** I really enjoy spending time by myself. I like walking around, visiting an exhibition, running my errands, and then getting home and thinking: "I've shut the door, that's it, the world stays outside and I want nothing more to do with it." Then again, I do enjoy being with people, I enjoy chatting: I'm someone who loves having long conversations, but I also really value being alone and not feeling the pressure of having to socialize by force.

**Interviewer:** So regarding the amount of time you spend with others, you feel satisfied?

**Retiree:** Yes, it suits me just fine. It may happen that sometimes I think I could have done something more, like going to a concert, but I don't beat myself up over those things.

**Interviewer:** Thinking about your daily habits—such as nutrition, physical activity, and sleep—how do you organize yourself throughout the week?

**Retiree:** I have things I routinely do, and those are what I do; I chose them for myself. If I do Pilates, great; if I wake up one morning and don't feel like it, I don't do it. I have never been rigid. I chose to do Pilates so that I keep moving, even though there are periods when I don't feel like it and force myself to do it, because I know that otherwise, if you don't move, you get rusty.

**Interviewer:** Regarding meals, do you pay particular attention to them?

**Retiree:** Not overly so. I buy what I want, but I cook less and less, and in an increasingly simplified way. I no longer enjoy dedicating time to elaborate dishes: for many years I loved cooking and eating unique dishes, but now I'd rather flip through a magazine than stand at the stove making an intricate recipe. You eat just the same anyway. Besides, with age, minor health issues come along that require you to watch what you can or cannot eat.

**Interviewer:** And what about the quality of the food?

**Retiree:** I try to eat wholesome things; I don't like low quality. I eat foods that are less seasoned and less elaborate, but genuine. This summer I walked into a budget discount store by chance after hearing the advertisements: I went in to pick up two things and came out saying that I couldn't buy anything in there. Everything was over-seasoned, over-processed, full of additives; it might be cheap, but I would find it horribly indigestible. I prefer going to the market and buying fruit and vegetables directly from farmers, being mindful to spend reasonably without demanding out-of-season produce at exorbitant prices.

**Interviewer:** So you pay close attention to product origin.

**Retiree:** Yes, you need to pay a bit of attention. Even with farmers I stay alert: if I hear one complaining because they can't use glyphosate like they do in America, I immediately switch stalls! Also, I like trying different cuisines: if there's a period where I don't feel like cooking, I get Indian takeaway or something else; I don't have the rigid dogma of having to eat strictly Italian food or the same old pasta. If green beans cost 12 euros a kilo at the prime-produce stalls, I simply go to the other side of the market where they cost 3 or 4 euros.

**Interviewer:** Speaking of sleep instead, how have you been resting lately? What promotes or disrupts your rest?

**Retiree:** The quality of my sleep disappeared a long time ago! What primarily disrupts me is noise. For the winter I resolved it by having triple glazing installed on the windows, but in summer with the windows open you hear the traffic and it's a relentless nuisance. Fortunately, there is no nightlife street party scene beneath my building; otherwise, I'd have to emigrate! Loud noises irritate me regardless of whether I need to sleep or not. The deep sleep I used to have, which I called "stone sleep," is a distant memory: I wake up poorly rested. I don't take medications or substances to sleep: if I sleep, I sleep; if I don't sleep, I read or get up and look out the window until I feel sleepy again. Even reading in bed barely works anymore: sometimes I think I'm drifting off, turn off the light, and in that very second I'm wide awake again.

**Interviewer:** Have you ever considered trying audiobooks to fall asleep or relax?

**Retiree:** I thought about it, but they're not for me. When walking in the street I must have one ear open to hear ambient noises, whether a car is approaching or what's happening around me. At home it doesn't come naturally to use them: I have a bookcase filled with volumes accumulated over an entire lifetime, and I prefer reading a paper book, exactly the way I buy a physical newspaper every day. I am the narrator of my own eyes; the day I can no longer see, perhaps then I'll fall back on audiobooks.

**Interviewer:** Regarding physical exercise, where do you take your Pilates classes?

**Retiree:** I do online classes at home. I do it out of laziness: online I don't even have to get dressed and travel to the gym! Also because in the morning it takes me quite a while to get going: I'm not one of those people who jump out of bed at six in the morning full of energy; I get up, sit down somewhere else, and wait until eight o'clock arrives. Besides Pilates, I enjoy walking, but there's a bit of laziness there too.

**Interviewer:** How do you usually get around the city?

**Retiree:** At a certain point I grew tired of driving, so I quit and sold the car outright. For someone who rarely uses it, a car has exorbitant costs between road tax, insurance, and maintenance regardless of the mileage driven. I get around on foot. I would love to walk in the hills or in green spaces; walking downtown while looking at shop windows is good for the soul, but it's not real physical workout.

**Interviewer:** How do you feel about living in your home and in your neighborhood?

**Retiree:** In my home I feel wonderful; it suits all my needs. I have lived in the neighborhood all my life: the positive aspect is that it is close to downtown and allows me to get around on foot in total autonomy. What I find inconvenient and dislike, however, is how it is changing: almost all traditional neighborhood shops have vanished, and many areas have been converted into nighttime party venues, which I don't frequent in the slightest. In the apartment building life is normal: you get along with some, find others less likeable—dynamics common to any building.

**Interviewer:** If you face a bad day, a worry, or an unexpected practical problem, how do you handle it? Who do you turn to?

**Retiree:** As long as I can manage on my own, I don't turn to anyone. If I truly need help I call someone, and if it were an extreme emergency I'd dial 112, hoping I could reach the phone and unlock the front door. Faced with a setback, I might get angry in the moment if it annoys me, but I don't build up exaggerated anxiety: my only real concern is the possibility of falling gravely ill; other than that, they're just ordinary life matters.

**Interviewer:** And when good news or a pleasant moment happens, who do you share it with?

**Retiree:** Either with my sister or with a close female friend; they are the people I habitually turn to.

**Interviewer:** Turning to technology: could you tell us about the last time you used a smartphone, computer, or an application for practical tasks or information?

**Retiree:** I use my phone every day to look up information, and in fact I think I spend far too much time on it! The risk is that you jump from one piece of news to another and realize half the day has slipped by without getting anything done. I don't like using applications: I vastly prefer browsing traditional websites via a browser. Unfortunately, many websites are no longer updated because everyone is pushing toward apps, but how did we live before without apps? The old printed Yellow Pages were an exceptional resource. On modern websites, the thing I despise most is the question at the bottom: "Was this page helpful?": they are almost always generic pages lacking the concrete answers you're looking for.

**Interviewer:** And what about navigation or maps like Google Maps, how do you find that?

**Retiree:** I struggle with it. The old paper street atlas of the city where you searched the street directory was infinitely faster and more efficient than Google Maps! I still jealously keep one. I have barely used Google Maps: it irritates me because it calculates routes its own way and does what the application wants rather than what I want. I started using computers late, around age 50; I took courses, learned to use them, and I'm naturally curious, but as far as I can get on my own, I manage; otherwise, I let it go. When my sister told me you just needed to enter satellite coordinates, I was dumbfounded: I'm old-fashioned, certain things just get on my nerves.

**Interviewer:** Looking at your experience as a retiree, what aspects do you find most rewarding, and which ones most demanding?

**Retiree:** I find almost nothing demanding, other than the physical aches and pains of aging. The most rewarding thing of all is not having to work while being able to support oneself: I don't have a huge pension, but it is enough to live peacefully and do whatever I want with my time.

**Interviewer:** Is there anything else about your daily life or your wellbeing that you would like to add?

**Retiree:** No, I really wouldn't say so, I've chatted more than enough!

**Interviewer:** We thank you very much for your availability and kindness. Your account will be extremely valuable for our research.

**Retiree:** Thank you, and all the best with your project!

### General Summary

The interviewee is a retiree living alone in Turin, in a historically residential neighborhood close to the city center. She describes herself with sharp self-deprecation as someone deliberately non-hyperactive, asserting her right to "do nothing" and live free of rigid schedules or alarm clocks after decades spent in boring jobs. While cherishing domestic quiet and solitude once she closes the front door, she maintains an intimate social circle (her sister and a trusted female friend) and enjoys spontaneous conversations. She preserves her physical wellbeing by taking online Pilates classes from home purely for logistical convenience and walks regularly after selling her car for financial and practical reasons. She places great emphasis on food quality and healthiness (buying from local farmers at the open market, rejecting ultra-processed and industrial goods), while coping with chronic insomnia aggravated by urban noise and summer traffic. Her relationship with digital technology is ambivalent: she spends substantial daily time on her smartphone reading news, but rejects the proliferation of dedicated apps in favor of traditional web browsers and prefers analog tools (paper newspapers, physical books, printed street maps) due to the perceived rigidity and lack of transparency of apps like Google Maps.

### Salient Points

1. **Reclaiming Time and Asserting the Right to "Do Nothing"**

   * **Freedom from the alarm clock:** Considers the absence of binding schedules and morning alarm clocks the most rewarding achievement of retirement, following a career spent in boring or grueling jobs.
   * **Rejection of senior hyperactivity:** Openly claims her own laziness and feels no obligation to fill her days with structured activities; asserts her freedom to rest on the sofa free of guilt.
2. **Physical Wellbeing, Health, and Movement Strategies**

   * **Low-friction physical exercise:** Follows online Pilates classes from home to eliminate travel to the gym and accommodate her slow morning awakening rhythm, aware that physical activity is essential to keep from getting "rusty."
   * **Pedestrian mobility and vehicle elimination:** Stopped driving and sold her car to eliminate fixed ownership overheads (insurance, road tax, maintenance); commutes entirely on foot toward downtown for daily errands and newspapers.
   * **Insomnia and acoustic sensitivity (Pain Point):** Suffers from early awakenings and fragmented sleep; installed triple glazing for winter, yet summer street traffic noise with open windows severely disrupts rest. Rejects sleeping pills and audiobooks, preferring physical books.
3. **Mindful Nutrition and Wholesome Eating**

   * **Culinary simplification:** Has reduced the time spent cooking elaborate meals, favoring quick, wholesome preparations.
   * **Strict ingredient selection:** Categorically refuses discount grocery chains offering ultra-processed or additive-laden items; buys produce from farmers at the municipal market, watching both fair pricing (rejecting speculative out-of-season produce) and growing methods (rejection of pesticides/glyphosate).
   * **Gastronomic openness:** Regularly explores diverse international cuisines (e.g., Indian takeaway) when not in the mood to cook.
4. **Urban Space and Neighborhood Transformations**

   * **Hyperlocal walkability:** Appreciates her home's proximity to Turin's city center, allowing effortless walking access for daily errands.
   * **Gentrification and nightlife (Pain Point):** Laments the extinction of traditional mom-and-pop shops and the conversion of the neighborhood into an active nightlife district, which she never attends and finds a primary source of acoustic disturbance.
5. **Relationship with Technology: Information Overload and Analog Preference**

   * **Browser vs. App preference:** Daily smartphone user for news reading (acknowledging the risk of losing half a day scrolling), but resists the mandatory installation of single-purpose apps, preferring open web pages via mobile browser.
   * **Distrust and friction with algorithmic workflows:** Finds Google Maps unintuitive and manipulative ("it does what the application wants rather than what I want"); favors printed city maps and books, which she perceives as transparent, immediate tools.
   * **Independent troubleshooting:** Solves technical hurdles alone up to her skill level; when facing insurmountable software friction, she prefers abandoning the task over delegating.
